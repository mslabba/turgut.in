How Travel Agency Software Works: Itineraries, Bookings and Supplier Rates

A customer asks for a five-day trip to Dubai: flights, a hotel near the beach, airport transfers and a desert safari. Ten years ago, the agent would have phoned four suppliers and typed the prices into a document. Today, one screen searches live fares from hundreds of airlines, checks hotel rooms from several suppliers, builds a day-by-day itinerary, takes payment and issues the tickets. That is what travel agency software does: it connects the agency to the world's travel inventory, turns the results into a trip a customer can understand,

The three layers of travel agency software

Most travel agencies do not run on one system. They run on three layers that pass the same booking from one to the next. The search and booking layer is where the agent works: it searches flights, hotels, transfers, activities and visas, compares prices across suppliers, and confirms the booking. The selling layer is what the customer sees: the agency's own website, where travellers book directly, or a B2B portal where sub-agents book at net rates against a credit limit. Both run on the same inventory, so the price an agent quotes and the price a customer sees online never disagree. The back-office layer handles the money: invoices, receipts, supplier payments, commissions and reconciliation — the least visible layer, and the one that saves the most time.

Where supplier rates come from

The rooms and seats belong to suppliers, and the software's job is to reach them all through one search.

Global distribution systems (GDS) are the oldest and largest source. Three networks — Amadeus, Sabre and Travelport — aggregate live fares and availability from hundreds of airlines, hotel chains and car rental companies. An agent searches once and sees results from every connected supplier. Access is professional: agencies hold an office ID with a GDS, or buy access through a host agency or consolidator.

NDC and direct airline connections are the newer channel. Airlines now sell some fares directly through their own APIs, using a standard called NDC, and low-cost carriers — which never joined the GDS — sell only this way. A modern booking engine checks both side by side, because the cheapest fare for a route may sit in either place.

Hotel bed banks do for hotels what the GDS does for flights: they buy rooms in bulk and resell them to agencies at wholesale rates. Direct contracts are the rates an agency negotiates itself — a hotel chain's fixed seasonal rate, or a local transfer company's set price per trip — stored next to the API suppliers, under the same markup and payment rules. This matters because a direct contract is often where the agency's margin lives.

How a booking actually flows

Every booking follows the same five steps inside the software. Search: the agent enters dates, destination and travellers, and the engine queries every connected supplier at once, then normalises the different answers — currencies, fare names, room types — into one comparable list. Price: the system applies the agency's markup and currency rules to the supplier's net rate, so the price the customer sees already includes the agency's margin, with taxes calculated from the fare rules. Hold and book: when the customer confirms, the software sends the booking back to the supplier. For a flight this creates a PNR — a Passenger Name Record — holding passenger details, flight segments, fare and ticketing deadline. For a hotel it creates a confirmation with a reference number and cancellation terms. Pay: the customer pays by card or bank transfer, or the booking is charged against a sub-agent's wallet or credit limit. Document: the system issues the ticket, the hotel voucher and the itinerary. If a flight changes, the PNR updates and the documents are reissued.

The itinerary builder: turning bookings into a trip

A list of confirmations is not a trip. The itinerary builder is where the booking becomes something the customer can follow: day by day, hour by hour. The builder pulls the confirmed bookings in automatically and the agent adds the detail around them: meeting points, contact numbers, check-in times, what is included and what is not. The result is a branded proposal the customer approves online, and then a final document they carry on the trip, often in a mobile app with offline access.

The back office: money, commissions and reconciliation

Every booking becomes an invoice the moment it is confirmed, and commissions — what the supplier owes the agency, and what the agency owes its sub-agents — are calculated from the booking, not worked out at month end from memory. Then comes reconciliation: the software matches each supplier statement line against a booking and flags the ones that do not match — a hotel that charged the wrong rate, a commission that never arrived. Without this, the leakage is invisible: a few per cent of revenue, every month, in amounts too small to notice one by one.

What to look for in travel agency software

If you are choosing a system, four things matter most. Supplier breadth: does it reach the GDS, direct airline content, bed banks and your own contracts in one search, One record: does a trip live in a single record from first quote to final refund? Markup and currency control: can you set different markups per supplier, per channel and per customer, and quote in the customer's currency? Reconciliation: does it match supplier statements to bookings automatically,

Frequently asked questions

What is a GDS, and does a small agency need one?

A GDS is a global distribution system — a network aggregating live fares and availability from hundreds of airlines, hotels and car rental companies into one search. A small agency does not need its own GDS contract: access usually comes through a host agency, a consolidator or a booking platform that holds the credentials.

What is NDC, and how is it different from GDS fares?

NDC is a newer standard that lets airlines sell directly to agencies and booking platforms through their own APIs, instead of through a GDS. Some fares and extras only exist on the direct channel, so an engine that checks both finds options a GDS-only search would miss.

What is a PNR?

A PNR, or Passenger Name Record, is the central booking record created when a flight is booked. It holds the passenger details, flight segments, fare information, ticketing deadline and special requests such as meal preferences. Every change to the booking is recorded against the same PNR.

How do travel agencies make money on a booking?

Mostly through markup and commission. The agency buys at a supplier's net rate and sells at a higher price, keeping the difference, The software applies the markup automatically and tracks the commission owed, so the margin on each booking is visible instead of guessed.
