Build vs Buy an AI Agent Desk: A Practical Checklist for SaaS Teams

Buy an off-the-shelf AI agent when your use case is generic (support FAQs, scheduling, inbox triage) and the tool already connects to your systems. Build a custom agent when it must work inside your product, act on your own database with your permission model, or become a feature your customers pay for. The deciding factors are data access, permissions and ownership, not the model.

Below is the checklist we use with SaaS teams. Score each line for your use case.

Does the agent need your live, private data?

If the agent only needs public documentation or a help centre, many hosted tools handle that well. If it needs to read and act on live records in your database, with your tenant boundaries, a bought tool usually means exporting data to it or granting broad API access. That is often where build wins. Our Admin AI Agent for ConnectEdu is an example of the build case: it answers from live school data inside the product.

Who controls permissions?

Ask: can the tool enforce your roles and per-customer isolation server-side, or does it rely on prompts? If you sell multi-tenant SaaS, one leaked record across tenants is a serious incident. Buy only if the vendor's permission model matches yours.

How many integrations does it need?

One or two standard integrations (CRM, helpdesk, calendar) favour buying. Several internal systems, custom APIs or legacy databases favour building, because you will spend the integration effort either way.

What will it cost to run, not just to start?

Bought tools are often priced per seat, per conversation or per resolution. Custom agents carry model usage, hosting and maintenance. Estimate monthly volume honestly before comparing.

Who owns the agent and its improvements?

If the agent is a differentiator in your product, owning the code, prompts, evaluations and data flow matters. If it is an internal convenience, renting is fine.

How will you know it works?

Production agents need evaluation: a set of real questions with expected outcomes, logging of tool calls, and a human handoff for low-confidence cases. Check whether a bought tool lets you see why it did what it did. If you build, plan evaluation from day one.

Who supports it when it breaks?

A vendor gives you a support queue. A build gives you control but needs an owner, in-house or a partner.

The checklist

Question Leans buy Leans build
Data needed Public docs, FAQs Live private records
Permissions Simple roles Multi-tenant, fine-grained
Integrations 1–2 standard apps Several internal systems
Running cost Low, predictable volume High volume where per-seat pricing hurts
Ownership Internal convenience Product feature or differentiator
Evaluation Vendor dashboards suffice You need your own test set and logs
Support Vendor queue is fine You want direct control

Mostly left column: buy. Mostly right: build. A mix often means buy now for the generic part and build the product-critical part.

Frequently asked questions

Should we build or buy an AI agent?

Buy for generic use cases with standard integrations. Build when the agent must use your live private data, enforce your permissions, or be part of your product.

Is building an AI agent more expensive than buying?

Not always. Building costs more upfront; buying can cost more at high volume because of per-seat or per-conversation pricing. Compare total running cost.

What is the biggest risk of buying an AI agent tool?

Weak permission control over your data, especially in multi-tenant SaaS where data must stay isolated per customer.

Can we combine buying and building?

Yes. Many teams buy for generic tasks and build the product-critical agent that works on their own data.
