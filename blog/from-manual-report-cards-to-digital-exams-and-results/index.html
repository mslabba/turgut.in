From Manual Report Cards to Digital Exams and Results

Every term ends the same way in most schools: teachers hunched over mark sheets, adding columns by hand, while the office waits to type the totals into a spreadsheet, print the report cards, and hand them to children to carry home. Weeks pass between the last exam and the day a parent finally sees the result. In Connectedu, the same term ends differently: the teacher enters each mark once, totals and grades calculate themselves, and the report card reaches the parent's phone the moment the school publishes it. The exam did not change. The chain of hands it passes through disappeared.

The manual report card chain

A manual report card is not one job — it is a chain of jobs, and every link is done by a different person. The subject teacher marks the answer scripts and writes the marks on a mark sheet. The class teacher collects the sheets from every subject, copies the marks into a master register, and adds them up. The office retypes the totals into a spreadsheet or a printed format, applies the grading scale, and checks for mistakes. Then the cards are printed, sorted class by class, and sent home in school bags.

For a school with 400 students and eight subjects, that is 3,200 marks to copy at least twice. A single slip — a 78 written as 87, a total added wrong — travels silently down the chain and lands in front of a parent as fact. And because the chain is slow, the school cannot afford to run it often: parents see their child's progress two or three times a year, and nothing in between.

Where the manual chain breaks

The first break is time. Between the last exam and the published report card, most schools lose two to four weeks to marking, totalling, checking and printing. During those weeks, teachers cannot tell a parent how their child did, because the numbers are not final yet.

The second break is the lost card. A report card handed to a ten-year-old travels home in a bag, and sometimes it never arrives. The parent who most needs to see it is the one most likely to never know it was sent.

Exams in Connectedu: from timetable to answer script

The school sets up the exam once — the timetable, the subjects, the maximum marks for each paper, and the grading scale it follows, whether that is a percentage, a grade, or a board format. Teachers then build their papers from a question bank: questions written once, tagged by subject and topic, reused across class tests, unit tests and final exams instead of retyped every term.

Exams can run on paper in the hall or online through the platform, with timed delivery and automatic marking for objective questions. Either way, the result lands in the same place: the teacher enters the marks against each student, once, and that entry is the record every later step uses. There is no mark sheet to copy, no master register to fill, and no second round of typing in the office.

Marks entered once, totals automatic

The moment the teacher saves the marks, Connectedu does the arithmetic. Totals, percentages and grades are calculated by the system from the grading scale the school set — not added by hand, not retyped from a sheet. If a teacher corrects a mark, the total and the grade update on their own; nobody has to chase the correction through three documents.

That single change removes the biggest source of error in the whole chain. It also removes the waiting: the class teacher can see the class result the day marking finishes, instead of waiting for the office to finish totalling. Rank lists, subject averages and pass percentages are ready the same day.

The report card parents actually see

When results are ready, the school publishes them — and publishing is a single action, not a print run. The report card is generated from the marks already in the system, in the school's own format, with subject marks, totals, grades, attendance and the teacher's remarks. The parent gets a notification in the parent app and opens the card on their phone, the same day.

Nothing is lost in a school bag. The card a parent sees is the same card the teacher entered, with no retyping in between — so the total always matches the subject marks, and a correction made after publishing updates the card the parent sees. Parents can also look back at earlier terms in the same place, and see whether their child is improving or slipping, instead of digging out last year's paper card from a drawer.

What the school learns from the data

The quietest benefit is that results stop being a document and start being information. Because every mark lives in one system, the school can see patterns a paper chain could never show: the subject a whole class struggled with, the student whose maths marks have slipped for three terms in a row, the section that needs extra support before the board exam.

Teachers can spot a struggling student in week three of the term, from class test results, instead of discovering it at the parent-teacher meeting after the final exam. That is the real difference digital exams and results make — not the speed of the card, but the fact that the school can act while there is still time to help.

Frequently asked questions

Do parents get results the same day the school publishes them?

Yes. The moment the school publishes the results, parents get a notification in the parent app and can open the report card on their phone. There is no printing, no sorting and nothing to carry home in a school bag.

What happens if a mark needs correcting after results are published?

The teacher corrects the mark in one place and the total, grade and report card update on their own. The parent sees the corrected card — there is nothing to reprint and no second version to chase.

Can the report card match our board's format?

Yes. The school sets its own grading scale and report card format — percentage, grades or a board format — and the system applies it to every card automatically.

Do teachers have to learn new software to enter marks?

Very little. The teacher opens the class list, types each student's mark and saves. If a teacher can use a phone, they can enter marks — and they never have to add up totals or copy marks into a second register again.
