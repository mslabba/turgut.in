How Parking Management Software Works: Slots, Payments and Number-Plate Recognition

Parking management software runs a car park from the moment a car reaches the gate to the moment it leaves. It holds entry and exit events, slot occupancy, tariffs, payments, permits and enforcement in one system. Without it, a busy car park runs on a guard and a paper log — queues build at the barrier, drivers circle looking for a slot and revenue leaks through unrecorded stays.

How entry and exit work: barriers, tickets and touchless gates

A car park takes vehicles through one of three entry styles. The oldest is the ticket: a driver presses a button, takes a barcode ticket and the barrier lifts. At the exit the ticket is scanned, the fee is calculated from the entry time and the barrier opens once payment is made. The second style is a card or tag: season parkers tap an access card or carry a long-range RFID tag.

The third style is touchless. A camera reads the number plate, the software checks it against its lists and the barrier opens on its own. Most sites mix all three: plates for regulars, tickets for casual visitors and cards for staff. The software treats each as the same thing — an entry event with a time, a lane and a vehicle identity.

How number-plate recognition works: from camera to decision

Automatic number-plate recognition, or ANPR, is the engine behind touchless parking. A sensor — usually a ground loop or a radar trigger — tells the camera a vehicle is in position. The camera captures several frames of the plate, often with infrared light so it works at night and in rain. The software then finds the plate inside the frame, straightens it and reads the characters with optical character recognition, combining several frames to settle on one reading. Well-lit systems reach 95 to 99 percent accuracy; heavy rain, fog or a dirty plate can still defeat them, which is why every good system has a fallback.

Finally, the plate becomes an event: the software checks it against its lists — subscribers, staff, guests, a block list — and decides whether to open the barrier or flag the vehicle. The plate, time, lane and a photo are stored together as evidence.

How slot occupancy is tracked: sensors, signs and guidance

Knowing how many cars entered is not the same as knowing which slots are free. That is the job of bay sensors. An ultrasonic sensor above each bay measures the distance to the floor; a car underneath shortens it. In-ground magnetic sensors detect the metal of a parked vehicle, and overhead cameras run a vision model that classifies each marked bay as occupied or empty.

The software turns those states into guidance. Signs at the entrance and on each level show how many slots are free, and a driver app can show the same count before arrival. For the operator, the same data shows which levels fill first and which bays never turn over.

How payments and pricing work: tariffs, validations and autopay

Every stay becomes a session with an entry time and an exit time, and the tariff turns that duration into a fee. A typical setup has a free first period, hourly rates up to a daily cap, evening and weekend rates, and special rates for events — plus validations, where a shop or office stamps a stay so the driver pays less or nothing.

Payment happens in several places. A pay station takes cards and cash before the driver returns to the car. A mobile app or a QR code at the exit lets the driver pay by phone. And with ANPR, the most convenient option is autopay: the plate is linked to a payment method, so the fee is collected automatically when the car leaves. Every payment is matched to its session, so the day's revenue reconciles against the entry and exit log.

Permits, subscriptions and enforcement

Not every car in a car park is a casual visitor. Offices, hospitals and residential blocks sell monthly permits: a subscriber registers a plate, pays by card or direct debit, and the plate becomes the permit. Renewals run automatically, and a lapsed payment removes the plate from the authorised list the same day. Guests work the same way: a resident or receptionist pre-registers a visitor's plate with a time window, and the guest drives straight in. Enforcement is automatic too: a plate with no valid session or an overstay is flagged in real time, without a patrol walking the rows.

What the operator sees: one dashboard for every site

The operator does not watch cameras; they watch numbers. A dashboard shows live occupancy by site, level and zone, entries and exits per hour, revenue so far today and open sessions. Over a month, the reports answer the questions that decide the business: revenue per slot, peak hours and average stay length.

Multi-site operators get all of this in one account. Each site keeps its own tariffs and permits, while head office compares occupancy and revenue across sites on the same screen. A site that fills by 9 a.m. is a signal to reprice or expand; a site at forty percent is a signal to market it.

What good parking software gets right

The best parking systems share four traits. First, one session record for everything — plate reads, tickets, tags and app payments all land in the same ledger. Second, live occupancy that drivers and operators both trust. Third, a payment flow that finishes before the driver reaches the exit. And fourth, an offline plan: cameras and barriers keep working when the network drops, and events sync when it returns.

Frequently asked questions

How accurate is number-plate recognition in a car park?

Modern systems reach 95 to 99 percent accuracy in good light. Rain, fog and dirty plates lower that, so a good system combines several frames and falls back to a ticket or a manual check when the read is uncertain.

Can a car park run without tickets or barriers at all?

Yes. ANPR cameras at the entry and exit create the session, and the driver pays by app or by autopay. It works best where enforcement is strong, because no physical gate stops a non-payer from leaving.

Does parking software support dynamic pricing?

Yes. Rates can change with live occupancy, time of day or events, subject to caps set by the operator. It needs trustworthy slot data, so it is usually paired with bay sensors or ANPR entry counts.

What happens when the internet goes down?

A well-built system keeps the barriers and cameras running locally. Entry and exit events are stored on site and synced to the cloud when the connection returns, each with its own timestamp, so no session or payment is lost.
